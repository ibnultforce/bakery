      <article class="imp-article">
        <p><strong>Every loaf of sourdough we bake starts with Margot</strong> — a bubbling jar of flour and water that has lived in our bakehouse for twelve years. People often ask whether they can buy a piece of her. (You can! Ask at the counter.) But there's real joy in growing your own, and it's simpler than you think.</p>
        <p>Here is the exact schedule we teach in our weekend bread classes.</p>
        <h2>What you'll need</h2>
        <ul>
          <li>A clean 1-litre glass jar with a loose lid</li>
          <li>Whole-wheat or whole-rye flour (to start) and unbleached white bread flour (for feeding)</li>
          <li>Filtered or room-temperature tap water left out overnight</li>
          <li>A kitchen scale — volume measurements make this much harder</li>
          <li>A rubber band to mark the starter's level</li>
        </ul>
        <div class="imp-tip-box"><strong>Baker's tip:</strong> Whole-grain flour carries more of the wild yeasts and bacteria you're trying to cultivate. Using it for the first two days gives your starter a head start.</div>
        <h2>The 7-day schedule</h2>
        <ol class="imp-timeline">
          <li><strong>Day 1 — Mix.</strong> Stir 50g whole-wheat flour with 50g water (about 26°C) into a thick paste. Cover loosely and leave somewhere warm.</li>
          <li><strong>Day 2 — Wait.</strong> You may see a few bubbles, or nothing at all. Both are normal. Give it a stir and leave it alone.</li>
          <li><strong>Day 3 — First feed.</strong> Discard all but 50g. Add 50g flour (half whole-wheat, half white) and 50g water. Mark the level with your rubber band.</li>
          <li><strong>Day 4 — The false rise.</strong> Your starter may double dramatically, then go quiet. That's the wrong bacteria burning out — keep feeding once a day.</li>
          <li><strong>Day 5 — Twice a day.</strong> Switch to two feeds, roughly 12 hours apart, with white flour only. Look for a sweeter, yoghurt-like smell.</li>
          <li><strong>Day 6 — Rhythm.</strong> It should now rise predictably within 4–8 hours of feeding and smell pleasantly sour.</li>
          <li><strong>Day 7 — Test it.</strong> If it reliably doubles within 6 hours and a spoonful floats in water, it's ready to bake with.</li>
        </ol>
        <blockquote>“A starter isn't an ingredient so much as a pet. Feed it, keep it warm, and it will reward you for years.”</blockquote>
        <h2>Troubleshooting</h2>
        <p><strong>Grey liquid on top?</strong> That's “hooch”, a sign your starter is hungry. Pour it off and feed more often.</p>
        <p><strong>Nothing happening?</strong> Temperature is usually the culprit. Try the top of your fridge or inside an oven with just the light on.</p>
        <p><strong>Pink or orange streaks?</strong> Unfortunately that's contamination. Start again with a freshly cleaned jar.</p>
        <h2>Keeping it alive</h2>
        <p>Once established, you can store your starter in the fridge and feed it once a week. Take it out the night before you bake, give it a feed, and it'll be lively by morning.</p>
