        <h1>The Secret Life of a Croissant: 3 Days, 27 Layers</h1>
        <div class="imp-post-meta">By Tomás Rivera · September 28, 2026 · 6 min read</div>
      </div>
    </section>
    <div class="imp-container">
      <div class="imp-article-cover"><img src="/images/blog-lamination.svg" alt="Cross-section of laminated dough beside a rolling pin and butter block" width="1200" height="600"></div>
      <article class="imp-article">
        <p>At 7am on a Saturday, our pastry case holds around 400 croissants. Most of them will be gone by ten. What our customers don't see is that each one of those crescents started its journey <strong>three days earlier</strong>.</p>
        <p>Here's what happens in between.</p>
        <h2>Day 1 — The détrempe</h2>
        <p>It starts with the <em>détrempe</em>: a simple dough of flour, milk, sugar, salt, yeast and a little butter. We mix it just enough to bring it together — overworking it now makes for tough pastry later — then wrap it and let it rest in the fridge overnight.</p>
        <p>Meanwhile, we prepare the butter block. We use a cultured butter with 82% butterfat, beaten and rolled into a perfect 19cm square between sheets of parchment. It needs to be cold but pliable, like modelling clay.</p>
        <div class="imp-tip-box"><strong>Why cultured butter?</strong> It's slightly tangy, which balances the sweetness of the dough, and its lower water content means crisper, more distinct layers.</div>
        <h2>Day 2 — Lamination</h2>
        <p>This is where the magic happens. The butter block is enclosed in the dough, then rolled and folded three times:</p>
        <ol>
          <li><strong>The lock-in:</strong> butter is sealed inside the dough like an envelope.</li>
          <li><strong>First fold (letter fold):</strong> rolled long, folded in thirds — 3 layers of butter.</li>
          <li><strong>Second fold:</strong> rest 30 minutes, roll, fold again — 9 layers.</li>
          <li><strong>Third fold:</strong> one final rest and fold — <strong>27 layers</strong>.</li>
        </ol>
        <p>Temperature is everything. If the butter gets too warm it melts into the dough; too cold and it shatters into shards. Our lamination room sits at a steady 16°C and we check the dough with a probe thermometer between every fold.</p>
        <blockquote>“You can't rush lamination. The dough tells you when it's ready — you just have to learn to listen.”</blockquote>
        <h2>Day 3 — Shape, proof, bake</h2>
        <p>At 3am the dough is rolled to exactly 4mm thick and cut into long triangles. Each one is gently stretched, notched and rolled into its crescent by hand — a skilled baker can shape about 300 an hour.</p>
        <p>The croissants then proof for around two and a half hours at 26°C, until they wobble like jelly when the tray is nudged. An egg wash, and into the oven at 190°C for 18 minutes.</p>
        <p>The water in the butter turns to steam, pushing the layers apart and creating that airy honeycomb. The sugars in the dough caramelise into a deep amber crust. And by 7am, they're in the case — still crackling as they cool.</p>
        <h2>How to enjoy one properly</h2>
        <ul>
          <li>Eat it within 6 hours of baking for peak flakiness.</li>
          <li>Day-old? Five minutes in a 180°C oven brings it back to life.</li>
          <li>Never microwave a croissant. Please.</li>
        </ul>
